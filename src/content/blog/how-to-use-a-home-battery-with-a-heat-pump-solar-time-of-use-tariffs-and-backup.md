<h2 dir="auto" data-section-id="47kljx" data-start="86" data-end="152">One Winter Day Explains the System Better Than a Wiring Diagram</h2>
<p dir="auto" data-start="154" data-end="598"><strong data-start="154" data-end="598">A home battery works best with a heat pump when the control strategy coordinates three things: when solar electricity is available, when grid electricity is cheapest, and when the house actually needs heat. The battery can shift low-cost or solar energy into expensive periods and provide backup during outages, but reserve SoC, inverter power, heat-pump demand, and available battery kWh determine how much flexibility is really available.</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="154" data-end="598"><img src="/assets/images/blog/how-to-use-a-home-battery-with-a-heat-pump-solar-time-of-use-tariffs-and-backup/1_349.jpg" alt="How to Use a Home Battery with a Heat Pump - Solar, Time-of-Use Tariffs, and Backup" width="400" height="400" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="600" data-end="887">Energy Saving Trust specifically recommends combining heat pumps with solar, batteries and time-of-use tariffs where appropriate, while its 2026 research found that tariff choice can materially change the economics of integrated home-energy systems.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="889" data-end="952">The easiest way to see why is to follow the house for 24 hours.</p>
<h2 dir="auto" data-section-id="bfekn0" data-start="954" data-end="1017">02:00 — Electricity Is Cheap, but Should the Battery Charge?</h2>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1019" data-end="1054">Assume a winter time-of-use tariff:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1056" data-end="1079"><strong data-start="1056" data-end="1079">Off-peak: $0.10/kWh</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1081" data-end="1100"><strong data-start="1081" data-end="1100">Peak: $0.32/kWh</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1102" data-end="1128">The battery is at 35% SoC.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1130" data-end="1179">Solar production tomorrow is forecast to be poor.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1181" data-end="1307">Charging the battery overnight may make sense because that energy can later support the heat pump during the expensive period.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1309" data-end="1354">But I would not automatically charge to 100%.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1356" data-end="1459">If tomorrow is sunny, filling the battery from the grid overnight could leave no room for midday solar.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1461" data-end="1509">The control strategy therefore needs to balance:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1511" data-end="1540"><strong data-start="1511" data-end="1540">tomorrow's solar forecast</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1542" data-end="1569"><strong data-start="1542" data-end="1569">expected heating demand</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1571" data-end="1589"><strong data-start="1571" data-end="1589">tariff periods</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1591" data-end="1609"><strong data-start="1591" data-end="1609">backup reserve</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1611" data-end="1828">Energy Saving Trust notes that batteries can be charged during low-cost tariff periods and then used to power a heat pump when electricity is more expensive—even without solar PV.</p>
<h2 dir="auto" data-section-id="xk8w1p" data-start="1830" data-end="1859">06:30 — The House Wakes Up</h2>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1861" data-end="1892">Outdoor temperature has fallen.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1894" data-end="1926">The heat pump is working harder.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1928" data-end="1955">Breakfast appliances start.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1957" data-end="2028">Now the battery faces a <strong data-start="1981" data-end="1990">power</strong> problem as well as an energy problem.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2030" data-end="2038">Suppose:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2040" data-end="2078"><strong data-start="2040" data-end="2078">Heat pump electrical input: 2.2 kW</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2080" data-end="2119"><strong data-start="2080" data-end="2119">Kitchen and household loads: 1.8 kW</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2121" data-end="2144"><strong data-start="2121" data-end="2144">Other loads: 0.5 kW</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2146" data-end="2152">Total:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2154" data-end="2164"><strong data-start="2154" data-end="2164">4.5 kW</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2166" data-end="2300">A 10 kWh battery may contain plenty of energy, but a 3 kW battery-inverter system cannot independently supply this entire 4.5 kW load.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2302" data-end="2346">That is why a Home ESS quotation needs both:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2348" data-end="2366"><strong data-start="2348" data-end="2366">kWh for energy</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2368" data-end="2371">and</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2373" data-end="2403"><strong data-start="2373" data-end="2402">kW for simultaneous power</strong>.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2405" data-end="2700">A real Energy Saving Trust case study illustrates this constraint: one household's battery inverter was limited to <strong data-start="2520" data-end="2553">2.6 kW charge/discharge power</strong>, restricting how much battery power could be used by the heat pump despite having battery storage available.</p>
<h2 dir="auto" data-section-id="1c459ec" data-start="2702" data-end="2740">11:30 — Solar Has Two Possible Jobs</h2>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2742" data-end="2770">Now rooftop PV is producing:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2772" data-end="2782"><strong data-start="2772" data-end="2782">4.2 kW</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2784" data-end="2800">The house needs:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2802" data-end="2812"><strong data-start="2802" data-end="2812">1.0 kW</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2814" data-end="2841">The heat pump is averaging:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2843" data-end="2853"><strong data-start="2843" data-end="2853">1.2 kW</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2855" data-end="2863">Surplus:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2865" data-end="2893"><strong data-start="2865" data-end="2893">4.2 − 1.0 − 1.2 = 2.0 kW</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2895" data-end="2914">That surplus could:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2916" data-end="2938"><strong data-start="2916" data-end="2938">charge the battery</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2940" data-end="2942">or</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2944" data-end="2967"><strong data-start="2944" data-end="2966">export to the grid</strong>.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2969" data-end="3011">Which is better depends partly on tariffs.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3013" data-end="3134">If exported solar earns nearly as much as electricity costs later, storing every surplus kWh may not make economic sense.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3136" data-end="3245">If evening electricity is much more expensive than the export value, retaining solar becomes more attractive.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3247" data-end="3315">This is why I would not program a Ruibit/Dawnice Home ESS simply as:</p>
<blockquote data-start="3317" data-end="3357">
<p dir="auto" data-start="3319" data-end="3357">Solar surplus = always charge battery.</p>
</blockquote>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3359" data-end="3450">The EMS/inverter strategy should understand what the stored electricity is being saved for.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3359" data-end="3450"><img src="/assets/images/blog/how-to-use-a-home-battery-with-a-heat-pump-solar-time-of-use-tariffs-and-backup/1_798.jpg" alt="How to Use a Home Battery with a Heat Pump - Solar, Time-of-Use Tariffs, and Backup" width="400" height="400" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></p>
<h2 dir="auto" data-section-id="fm5rso" data-start="3452" data-end="3502">15:00 — The House Itself Becomes Energy Storage</h2>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3504" data-end="3577">There is another useful tool that does not have a lithium cell inside it.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3579" data-end="3592">The building.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3594" data-end="3817">If electricity is cheap or solar is abundant before the evening peak, the heat pump may sometimes preheat the home slightly—within the homeowner's comfort preferences—rather than waiting until electricity becomes expensive.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3819" data-end="3884">A hot-water cylinder can provide another form of thermal storage.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3886" data-end="3944">This does <strong data-start="3896" data-end="3903">not</strong> mean aggressively overheating the house.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3946" data-end="4241">Heat pumps generally work efficiently with steady operation and lower flow temperatures rather than repeated high-temperature bursts. Energy Saving Trust recommends longer, steadier operation and appropriate weather compensation for efficient heat-pump use.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4243" data-end="4317">The battery and building therefore provide different kinds of flexibility:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4319" data-end="4357"><strong data-start="4319" data-end="4357">Battery → shifts electrical energy</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4359" data-end="4409"><strong data-start="4359" data-end="4409">Building/hot-water system → shifts heat demand</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4411" data-end="4449">A smart control strategy can use both.</p>
<h2 dir="auto" data-section-id="1cmehoq" data-start="4451" data-end="4506">17:30 — This Is the Hour the Battery Was Waiting For</h2>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4508" data-end="4539">Solar generation has collapsed.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4541" data-end="4588">The tariff has moved into its expensive period.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4590" data-end="4628">The heat pump still needs electricity.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4630" data-end="4658">Suppose the house now draws:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4660" data-end="4681"><strong data-start="4660" data-end="4681">Heat pump: 2.0 kW</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4683" data-end="4706"><strong data-start="4683" data-end="4706">Other loads: 0.8 kW</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4708" data-end="4714">Total:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4716" data-end="4726"><strong data-start="4716" data-end="4726">2.8 kW</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4728" data-end="4775">If the battery supplies 2.8 kW for three hours:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4777" data-end="4798"><strong data-start="4777" data-end="4798">2.8 × 3 = 8.4 kWh</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4800" data-end="4965">A nominal 10 kWh battery may or may not provide that full amount as usable AC energy after considering its usable SoC window, conversion losses, and reserve setting.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4967" data-end="5029">So I would model the <strong data-start="4988" data-end="5012">evening load profile</strong>, not simply say:</p>
<blockquote data-start="5031" data-end="5082">
<p dir="auto" data-start="5033" data-end="5082">10 kWh battery = three hours of heat-pump backup.</p>
</blockquote>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5084" data-end="5417">Energy Saving Trust's 2026 modelling across more than one million scenarios found that households combining heat pumps, solar and batteries can make greater use of low-cost electricity, but savings vary substantially with tariff, heat-pump performance, household demand and system configuration.</p>
<h2 dir="auto" data-section-id="17crwws" data-start="5419" data-end="5468">21:00 — Stop Discharging or Keep Saving Money?</h2>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5470" data-end="5494">Battery SoC has reached:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5496" data-end="5503"><strong data-start="5496" data-end="5503">30%</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5505" data-end="5548">The peak tariff continues for another hour.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5550" data-end="5585">Should the system keep discharging?</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5587" data-end="5629">That depends on what the homeowner values.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5631" data-end="5730">If the objective is maximum tariff savings, the battery may continue toward its normal minimum SoC.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5732" data-end="5798">If the homeowner wants outage protection, the system may preserve:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5800" data-end="5825"><strong data-start="5800" data-end="5825">20–30% backup reserve</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5827" data-end="5853">or another selected value.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5855" data-end="5900">That reserved energy has an opportunity cost.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5902" data-end="6045">A battery cannot simultaneously use its final kWh to avoid expensive grid electricity <strong data-start="5988" data-end="5995">and</strong> keep that same kWh untouched for a future outage.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6047" data-end="6113">This trade-off should be explained when quoting backup capability.</p>
<h2 dir="auto" data-section-id="1x6shwp" data-start="6115" data-end="6145">23:15 — Then the Grid Fails</h2>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6147" data-end="6185">Now the system design becomes visible.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6187" data-end="6224">The battery may have 3 kWh remaining.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6226" data-end="6255">What happens next depends on:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6257" data-end="6302"><strong data-start="6257" data-end="6302">whether backup/EPS operation is supported</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6304" data-end="6336"><strong data-start="6304" data-end="6336">which circuits are protected</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6338" data-end="6365"><strong data-start="6338" data-end="6365">battery discharge power</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6367" data-end="6400"><strong data-start="6367" data-end="6400">heat-pump startup requirement</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6402" data-end="6429"><strong data-start="6402" data-end="6429">remaining usable energy</strong></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6431" data-end="6476">and whether PV can operate during the outage.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6478" data-end="6645">DOE identifies backup power, solar-energy shifting and time-of-use management as reasons homeowners may consider battery storage.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6647" data-end="6696">But "battery backup" does not automatically mean:</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6698" data-end="6752"><strong data-start="6698" data-end="6751">whole-home heat-pump backup for the entire outage</strong>.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6754" data-end="6861">For a Ruibit/Dawnice Home ESS proposal, I would define the protected loads and reserve strategy explicitly.</p>

<h2 dir="auto" data-section-id="1xvwnkw" data-start="0" data-end="7">FAQs</h2>
<h3 dir="auto" data-section-id="aayh35" data-start="9" data-end="51">1. Can a home battery run a heat pump?</h3>
<p dir="auto" data-start="53" data-end="290">Yes, if the <strong data-start="65" data-end="184">battery discharge power and inverter output are sufficient for the heat pump and other simultaneous household loads</strong>. Battery kWh determines available energy, while kW determines how much power can be supplied at one time.</p>
<h3 dir="auto" data-section-id="231vp" data-start="292" data-end="357">2. How can a home battery reduce heat pump electricity costs?</h3>
<p dir="auto" data-start="359" data-end="556">The battery can store <strong data-start="381" data-end="466">surplus solar energy or electricity purchased during low-cost time-of-use periods</strong> and discharge later when the heat pump is operating during more expensive tariff periods.</p>
<h3 dir="auto" data-section-id="1rkb43m" data-start="558" data-end="641">3. Should a home battery charge from the grid overnight when using a heat pump?</h3>
<p dir="auto" data-start="643" data-end="915">It can make sense when overnight electricity is inexpensive and high heating demand is expected the next day. However, the control strategy should also consider <strong data-start="804" data-end="914">solar forecasts, expected heating demand, tariff periods, and available battery capacity for daytime solar</strong>.</p>
<h3 dir="auto" data-section-id="17sehk6" data-start="917" data-end="975">4. How does backup reserve affect heat pump operation?</h3>
<p dir="auto" data-start="977" data-end="1267">Keeping part of the battery at a minimum reserve SoC provides energy for potential outages, but that energy cannot simultaneously be used for daily tariff savings. The reserve level should reflect the homeowner's preferred balance between <strong data-start="1216" data-end="1266">backup resilience and energy-cost optimization</strong>.</p>
<h3 dir="auto" data-section-id="3f9hte" data-start="1269" data-end="1342">5. What information is needed to size a home battery for a heat pump?</h3>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1344" data-end="1545" data-is-last-node="" data-is-only-node="">Use the <strong data-start="1352" data-end="1544">heat pump load profile and startup power, household interval consumption, PV generation, electricity tariff, usable battery kWh, battery/inverter kW, required backup loads, and reserve SoC</strong>.</p>
