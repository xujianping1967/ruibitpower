<p>Most battery damage is not visible on the pallet. It appears the first time the unit is asked to take a high current, weeks after receiving inspection has signed the delivery off. That gap between acceptance and failure is what the checks below are for.</p>
<p>This is a diagnostic sequence rather than a record of a specific shipment, written to apply to any incoming container.</p>

<h2>Start from the symptom</h2>
<p>Three symptoms repeat across home battery deliveries.</p>
<p>Scuffing at the base or a corner of the enclosure, which the installer accepts because the install date is fixed. A terminal pair that reads the correct open-circuit voltage but trips protection the first time the pack is charged at a high rate. Condensation visible inside a unit opened in a warm room after weeks in a cold container.</p>
<p>Each is a packaging finding rather than a product finding, and each points at a different part of the configuration.</p>
<h2>The first hypothesis is usually wrong</h2>
<p>The instinct is that the unit was dropped. A unit actually dropped on a corner announces itself immediately: deformed mounting points, a cracked base, a bracket that no longer sits flat.</p>
<p>The mechanism that produces late failures is quieter. The battery moves inside its own packaging. Strapping holds the carton to the pallet, not the enclosure to the carton, so if the internal restraint is absent or compressed, the mass travels a short distance on every acceleration and stops against its own terminal end. Nothing looks damaged, and the terminal area has taken repeated load.</p>
<p>The second instinct, that the battery is simply poor quality, is not a finding. It closes an investigation without producing an action.</p>

<p>Worked example. The same drop height applied to a heavier unit produces proportionally more force at the point of contact, so a packing design proven on a 55 kg wall unit is not on the same basis as one needed for a 320 kg all-in-one. If a supplier shows one drop-test report and applies it across the whole range, the smallest unit is covered and the heaviest is not. Ask which mass the configuration was approved for and on what handling assumption, because a report without that number is not compared on the same basis as the unit being shipped.</p>
<h2>Where the evidence has to come from</h2>
<p>The site does not publish a packaging specification. The terms that would appear in one, including shock, vibration, desiccant, cushioning, foam and unpacking, do not appear anywhere on the product pages, so the approved configuration has to be requested from the supplier in writing rather than read off the datasheet.</p>
<p>Two documents are often mistaken for evidence of packaging design. The UN 38.3 transport test documentation covers the battery type and does not approve a packing method. A certificate covering the cell or the enclosure says nothing about how the two are restrained together. The  covers the paperwork side and is the place to start on classification and declaration.</p>
<p>Terminal protection has the shortest path to a serious outcome, because an unrestrained terminal that touches metal is a short circuit inside a container. It is also the easiest to verify: the terminal should be covered by an insulating cap or a fixed barrier, fitted so that it cannot fall off during handling.</p>
<p>Moisture follows a different route. Condensation forms when a cold unit is opened in a warm, humid room, and it forms on the coldest surface, the enclosure and the terminals. The published protection rating matters here: the wall-mounted units publish IP20 for indoor use, and the LumiSun-32K publishes IP54 for indoor and sheltered outdoor use. If a unit is opened and commissioned the day it leaves a cold container, the moisture arrives with it.</p>
<h2>The root cause is usually a change nobody reviewed</h2>
<p>The most frequent root cause is not a bad design. It is a good design changed for freight volume. A pilot shipment uses one approved packing configuration; production uses a thinner carton, a shorter pallet or one fewer internal restraint to fit more units per container. The change saves money per container and stays invisible until the claims arrive.</p>
<p>The second is at receiving. If the inspection records only the net weight on the label, packaging loss, moisture ingress and internal movement all become unmeasurable, because the number that would reveal them was never taken.</p>
<h2>Prevention, written into two documents</h2>
<p>In the purchase order, ask for the approved packing configuration reference, gross and net weight per unit, the terminal protection method, the moisture protection method, the stacking limit and the handling orientation. Gross weight is the figure to require, because net weight alone cannot show what the packing absorbed.</p>
<p>At receiving: photograph the packaging before opening anything, weigh the pallet and compare it with the declared gross weight, record serial numbers against the packing list, and reject rather than accept enclosure damage that reaches the base or a mounting point. A scuff on a face panel is cosmetic. Damage at a lifting or mounting point is structural, and it is irreversible once the unit is on the wall.</p>

<h2>FAQs</h2>
<p><strong>1. What should an importer check on a home battery delivery before signing it off?</strong></p>
<p>Photograph the packaging before opening anything, weigh the pallet and compare it with the declared gross weight, and check the terminal area for a fitted insulating cover. Record the serial numbers against the packing list. Net weight alone will not show what the packing absorbed during transit.</p>
<p><strong>2. Why does battery damage show up weeks after receiving inspection?</strong></p>
<p>Because the unit can move inside its own packaging without leaving a mark. Strapping usually holds the carton to the pallet rather than the enclosure to the carton, so any missing internal restraint lets the mass travel a short distance and stop against the terminal end on every acceleration.</p>
<p><strong>3. How much does packaging have to protect?</strong></p>
<p>More than a wall unit suggests. The published masses in this range run from about 55 kg for a 5 kWh wall unit to about 320 kg for the LumiSun-32K, and the same drop height produces proportionally more force on the heavier unit. A design proven on the lightest model is not on the same basis as one needed for the heaviest.</p>
<p><strong>4. Does a UN 38.3 test report approve the packaging?</strong></p>
<p>No. UN 38.3 covers transport testing of the battery type, not the packing method. A cell or enclosure certificate says nothing about how the two are restrained together. The approved packing configuration is a separate document, requested rather than assumed.</p>
<p><strong>5. Why does moisture matter in shipping?</strong></p>
<p>Condensation forms when a cold unit is opened in a warm, humid room, on the coldest surface, which is the enclosure and the terminals. The wall-mounted units publish IP20 for indoor use, and the LumiSun-32K publishes IP54 for indoor and sheltered outdoor use. Commissioning a unit the day it leaves a cold container carries that moisture into the installation.</p>
