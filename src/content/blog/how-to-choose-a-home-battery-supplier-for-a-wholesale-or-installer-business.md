<p><strong>Choosing a home battery supplier for a wholesale or installer business is not a price exercise, and the two suppliers that look most alike on paper are usually the hardest to separate. Delete the prices first, and what remains is a comparison of scope. Scope is where the two bids stop agreeing, and it is where the decision is actually made.</strong></p>

<h2>What this review covers, and what it leaves out</h2>
<p>The object of the review is not the cheapest enclosure. It is the party that will still be answering questions in year six. That narrows the review to four items: what sits inside the quoted scope, what is left outside it as a scope gap, what evidence exists for each claim, who owns each warranty clock, and what happens when a configuration changes after approval.</p>
<p>It does not cover price negotiation, because a price comparison before scope alignment compares two different products. It does not cover the destination market's grid connection rules either, which no supplier controls.</p>
<p>If two suppliers answer all four items the same way, the review has nothing to resolve and price is the only variable left.</p>
<h2>What each party to the decision needs</h2>
<p>The importer needs a landed cost, an MOQ that matches a first container, payment milestones tied to verifiable deliverables, and a warranty expressed as throughput rather than as a year count. The installer needs a configuration that ships as approved, a commissioning procedure that fits a working day, and a handover pack the household can read. The owner is not a party here, but the owner is the constraint: whatever is signed has to be explainable in year eight.</p>
<p>The published warranty lines show why the distinction matters. Some configurations publish ten years for the battery and five years for the inverter. Others state ten years without naming which components that covers, and in those cases the inverter sits inside the same enclosure. Two suppliers can therefore quote ten years and describe two different positions.</p>
<h2>Why two compliant suppliers deadlock</h2>
<p>Worked example. Supplier A ships 20 kWh as one integrated unit with a 10 kW inverter inside the enclosure, published at about 220 kg. Supplier B ships the same nominal 20 kWh as two wall-mounted 10 kWh units at about 95 kg each, with a separate hybrid inverter. Both are compliant. Both are published.</p>
<p>The deadlock is not quality. The two bids answer different site questions. Supplier A removes the battery-to-inverter interface and puts the inverter warranty inside the product. Supplier B keeps the inverter as a separate line item with its own clock and its own replacement path, and adds a second wall assessment, a second anchor set and a second connection. Neither is worse. The wall, the floor, the route into the building and whoever services the site decide between them.</p>

<h2>Where the trade-off actually lands</h2>
<p>Track record separates the two bids, and only when it is checkable. The published records for this product range name the inverter and the configuration of each build: three wall-mounted 10 kWh units on a Sunsynk inverter in the , completed 2026-03; five 15 kWh wall-mounted units on a Deye inverter in , 2026-06; and a 64 kWh rack system on a Deye 50 kW three-phase inverter in , 2026-03.</p>
<p>That is the level of detail a supplier has to reach. A supplier that cannot name the inverter and unit count of a comparable build is offering a capacity figure, not a record.</p>
<p>The records published under the Ruibit Energy name list the inverter and the unit count of each build and identify the company as an authorized distributor of the Dawnice range rather than its manufacturer. Locating that line is the first thing a wholesale buyer should do, because it decides who carries the warranty.</p>
<h2>Open items the decision leaves behind</h2>
<p>Three items are usually still open on the day a supplier is chosen.</p>
<p>The first is the warranty position for a part inside the enclosure. If the published line does not name components, ask which ones it covers, and put the answer in writing before the order rather than after the first fault.</p>
<p>The second is the change process. A different cell model, a different BMS firmware release or a different production site is a different configuration, and the evidence behind the first one does not transfer automatically. Those decisions cannot be changed later without new documents.</p>
<p>The third is what travels with the goods: the handover pack, the escalation path for an installer with twenty units in the field, and the spare part generation, all set at the point of approval.</p>
<p>A supplier decision is close to irreversible for the life of the platform. The mounting positions, the metering point and the grid registration follow the configuration that was approved, so changing supplier mid-generation means a second spare-part lineage. Reviewing scope, evidence, warranty and change control takes an afternoon. Reversing them takes a season.</p>

<h2>FAQs</h2>
<p><strong>1. What should be compared before price when choosing a home battery supplier?</strong></p>
<p>Four items: what sits inside the quoted scope, what is left outside it as a scope gap, what evidence exists for each claim, and who owns each warranty clock. Until those four line up, two quotations are describing two different products and the price difference carries no information.</p>
<p><strong>2. Why can two suppliers both quote ten years of warranty and still differ?</strong></p>
<p>Because the published wording differs. Some configurations state ten years for the battery and five years for the inverter inside the same enclosure; others state ten years without naming components. Ask which parts the ten years covers, and get the answer as a clause rather than a sentence.</p>
<p><strong>3. What track record should a supplier be able to produce?</strong></p>
<p>The inverter and the unit count of a comparable build, not a capacity figure alone. A published record that names the configuration, the inverter and the completion month can be checked. A record that names only kilowatt-hours cannot.</p>
<p><strong>4. Who carries the warranty when the inverter fails inside an integrated unit?</strong></p>
<p>It depends on whether the supplier is the manufacturer or an authorized distributor of the brand. That line changes who holds the warranty obligation and who merely forwarded it, so establish the relationship before signing rather than after a fault.</p>
<p><strong>5. What change requires new evidence after approval?</strong></p>
<p>A different cell model, a different BMS firmware release or a different production site. Each one makes a different configuration, and the evidence behind the original does not transfer automatically. Those decisions cannot be changed later without new documents.</p>
